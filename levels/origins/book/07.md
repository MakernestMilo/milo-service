The one idea

A machine that keeps its numbers can be asked about the past. But it can't keep all of them. There is only so much room inside it. So somebody has to choose how often it writes one down.

That choice decides what your week can show you. Write a number every ten seconds and you see everything, for a few hours. Write one every hour and you get a month, and you miss anything short.

How often should it write the number down?

First run, then every night

Before you leave it, set WRITE EVERY to 10 seconds and watch the notebook bar on the display for one minute. You can see it filling.

Now set it back to your real choice and watch again. It has barely moved. That is the trade. Now you have seen it, not just been told it.

EVERY NIGHT, FOR SEVEN NIGHTS

One line on the chart card before bed: when will tomorrow's coldest moment be, and how cold? Check it the next day.

It takes thirty seconds. It is the difference between waiting a week and running an experiment for a week.

BREAK IT ON PURPOSE · 12 MIN, SEVEN DAYS LATER
The moment your machine missed

Welcome back. First, draw the week.

Turn the dial to LOG. Each click moves one hour. For each day, plot four points on the chart card: 4 in the morning, 10 in the morning, 4 in the afternoon, 10 at night. Then scroll slowly through the whole day and mark anything that jumps. Draw it in your own handwriting. You will notice things drawing it that you would never notice reading it.

Now find something specific. The moment somebody opened a window. The AC coming on, or the heater. The front door left open.

If you chose 15 minutes, a short event is one lonely point, or it isn't there at all.

It happened, and your machine has no record of it. Not because it failed. Because of a number you chose on day one, before you knew what you were choosing.

Now run one afternoon at a much shorter choice and watch the same kind of event show up properly. Two versions of the same room, and neither is the whole truth.

FOR THE GROWN-UP

The nightly guess is what keeps this week alive in the maker's head. Ask about it at bedtime. Don't set it as a task. Don't tidy the machine away, and don't unplug it to charge something else. Losing the week means starting the chapter again.
